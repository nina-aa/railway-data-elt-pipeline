# Findings

Source: Fintraffic / Digitraffic railway API, CC-BY 4.0. 14 days,
2026-08-27 → 2026-09-09. Generated by `scripts/make_charts.py`.

**Analysis base:** 212,987 arrival events at commercial stops that actually
happened, with a plausible delay (`has_actual_time` and `is_plausible_delay`,
cancellations excluded). Network on-time rate (arrival delay < 5 min):
**95.3%**; mean arrival delay **+0.68 min**.

## 1. Which stations have the worst arrival punctuality?

Of ~324
stations with a commercial arrival in the window, only **111** have at
least 200 arrivals (213 are below that and are left out — with 14 days
of data a station with 30–40 arrivals can sit anywhere in the ranking on noise).

Among those 111, the network average is **95.3%** on time.
The worst are two groups: far-north long-distance stops at the end of long runs
(Kemi, Oulu — see Q5), and a cluster of outer-commuter stations on the congested
Helsinki–Riihimäki main line (Jokela, Saunakallio, Hyvinkää) where trains arrive
already late off that line:

- **Kemi asema** (KEM): 46% on time, mean +4.6 min, n=316
- **Lappeenranta** (LR): 50% on time, mean +5.9 min, n=220
- **Oulu asema** (OL): 57% on time, mean +5.5 min, n=416
- **Imatra tavara** (IMT): 73% on time, mean +3.2 min, n=225
- **Jokela** (JK): 79% on time, mean +3.6 min, n=1,211
- **Saunakallio** (SAU): 79% on time, mean +3.3 min, n=1,190

The best are inner commuter stations, where trains are new to their run and the
schedule has recovery margin:

- **Mäkkylä** (MÄK): 99% on time, n=2,727
- **Pitäjänmäki** (PJM): 99% on time, n=2,727
- **Valimo** (VMO): 99% on time, n=2,727
- **Lentoasema** (LEN): 99% on time, n=3,149
- **Leinelä** (LNÄ): 99% on time, n=3,149
- **Aviapolis** (AVP): 99% on time, n=3,149

Even 14 days is thin for the low-volume long-distance stops (n≈200–400): treat
the bottom of the list as "clearly below average", not as a precise order.

## 2. How does delay vary by hour of day?

There is a mild build-up through the operating day, but the swing is small
because punctual commuter trains dominate the counts.

- Quietest: around **04:00** local, mean **-0.18 min**.
- Worst: **12:00** local, mean **+0.97 min**.
- Overnight (02:00–04:00) the mean goes slightly negative — a handful of mostly
  freight and repositioning moves that run ahead of a nominal path.

Delay drifts up from ~06:00, plateaus across the middle of the day and the
afternoon peak, and eases after ~19:00. It is a build-up of perturbation over
the day rather than a sharp rush-hour spike.

## 3. Most common delay causes — by frequency vs by attributed minutes

Only **9,716** events carry a cause
code (1.0% of all events; ~4% of events delayed 5 min or more). Every populated
`causes` array in the window has exactly one element, so attribution is
unambiguous here.

**By frequency**, the top codes are:
- `E` Running ahead of schedule — 3,722 events
- `L` Traffic control / dispatching — 2,237 events
- `T` Track & infrastructure — 1,336 events
- `M` Passenger services — 926 events

**By total minutes attributed**, the order changes:
- `L` Traffic control / dispatching — 25,488 min across 2,237 events (11 min each)
- `T` Track & infrastructure — 8,024 min across 1,336 events (6 min each)
- `J` Delayed train formation — 6,908 min across 232 events (30 min each)
- `R` Trackwork / engineering possessions — 6,779 min across 381 events (18 min each)

Why they differ: `E` (Running ahead of schedule) is the
**single most frequent code — 3,722 occurrences — and contributes 0 delay
minutes** by definition. And `J` (train-formation delay) is comparatively rare
(232 events) but each one is large
(~30 min), so it climbs the
minutes ranking. Frequency counts incidents; minutes weight them by severity.

## 4. Do long-distance and commuter trains differ in punctuality?

Yes, clearly.

| category | realised arrivals | on-time | mean delay | 90th pct delay |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Commuter | 181,470 | **97.0%** | +0.88 min | 2 min |
| Long-distance | 23,844 | **85.9%** | +2.06 min | 6 min |

Commuter services are ~11 points more punctual. They run
short routes with little room to accumulate delay and recover quickly between
dense stops; long-distance trains traverse hundreds of km of partly single-track
line where a single conflict propagates for the rest of the run (see Q5).

## 5. Does delay accumulate along a route, or do trains recover?

Traced on **IC 265, Helsinki → Kemijärvi**, an overnight long-distance train with
14 commercial stops that ran all 14 nights (mean delay per stop over
the 14 days):

- PSL  Pasila asema           stop  1: +1.6 min
- TKL  Tikkurila asema        stop  2: -0.5 min
- RI   Riihimäki asema        stop  3: +6.5 min
- HL   Hämeenlinna            stop  4: +5.3 min
- TPE  Tampere asema          stop  5: +3.6 min
- PKO  Parkano                stop  6: +3.1 min
- SK   Seinäjoki asema        stop  7: -6.8 min
- KOK  Kokkola                stop  8: +1.5 min
- YV   Ylivieska              stop  9: +2.4 min
- OL   Oulu asema             stop 10: -0.4 min
- KEM  Kemi asema             stop 11: +8.8 min
- ROI  Rovaniemi              stop 12: +10.7 min
- MIS  Misi                   stop 13: +10.3 min
- KJÄ  Kemijärvi              stop 14: +4.1 min

Both happen. Delay **builds** from roughly on-time in the Helsinki region to a
peak around Riihimäki (~6 min), eases a little to
Tampere, then is **over-corrected** at Seinäjoki — so much timetable slack that
the train sits ~7 min *early*
there. North of Oulu it **re-accumulates** through the single-track sections
(worst ~11 min at Rovaniemi), before a padded final
leg brings it into Kemijärvi ~4 min down. Recovery margin is
built into the long-distance schedule at the major hubs; the sparse northern
network gives the train nowhere to catch up once past Oulu.

## 6. Weekday vs weekend

Almost no difference:

- Weekday: **95.3%** on time, mean +0.69 min (164,300 arrivals)
- Weekend: **95.5%** on time, mean +0.64 min (48,687 arrivals)

Despite ~25% fewer trains at weekends (Stage 1 profile), punctuality is flat.
Day to day, the window ranges from **92.6%** (2026-09-07) to
**96.6%** (2026-09-09); no single day collapsed, so the 14-day
picture is not distorted by one bad day.

## 7. Anything surprising

- **The most frequent delay code is "running early."** `E` / *Etuajassakulku*
  accounts for 3,722 of the 9,716
  coded events — more than any genuine disruption cause — and by construction adds
  zero delay minutes. Traffic control logs early running as diligently as lateness.
- **Weekends are not more punctual** despite far fewer trains — congestion is not
  the binding constraint on this network in this window.
- **The delay distribution is sharply peaked at zero**: median +0 min,
  95th percentile only +4 min, yet a thin tail reaches into the hundreds.
  Finnish rail is "usually exactly on time, occasionally very late" rather than
  "chronically a few minutes off".
- **Cargo trains have a strongly negative mean delay** (~-12 min) — they are
  pathed conservatively and take whatever slot opens up.
